Pathogens act within a host that is part of a population, a community and ultimately an ecosystem, and factors at all these levels influence host-pathogen interactions. For instance, the rate of contact among individuals will have an effect on the speed a pathogen is transmitted in the population. Environmental changes, including habitat degradation, may negatively impact the immune system of hosts, making them more vulnerable to disease. The interactions between hosts, pathogens and the environment are the subject of study of **disease ecology**. 

Many of the pathogens that cause disease in humans are zoonotic (i. e., they have an animal origin), coming from either wild or domestic animals. Sometimes domestic animals serve as an intermediate host that gets infected by a wild reservoir and can transmit the pathogen to humans. In Hendra virus infection, for example, fruit bats transmit the virus to horses that can then pass it on to humans. Interactions at wildlife-domestic-human interfaces such as this are an important part of the transmission pathway of zoonotic diseases. The drivers of such interactions are diverse, dynamic in space and time, and determined by the socio-ecological system, and understanding them better is required to improve risk assessment and surveillance of (re-)emerging zoonotic diseases.  
As postdoc and research associate at the [TETIS](https://umr-tetis.cirad.fr/en/) joint research unit, I was involved in projects studying pathogen transmission at the wildlife-domestic-human interface in Africa, looking at risk factors of pathogen transmission and modelling animal movement in potential reservoir hosts. 

<b><span style="color: #7f0000; font-size: 24px;">Ebola spillover to humans</span></b>

Ebola is a disease caused by viruses belonging to the *Orhtoebolavirus*, with an average mortality rate of 50%. The reservoir host has not been identified, but some species of bats are suspected. Also, contact with infected primates or consumption of bushmeat, such as duikers, has been reported as the origin of some of the outbreaks indicating that other mammals can be intermediate hosts. 
Outbreaks have been located mainly in central Africa, but in 2014 an outbreak that lasted two years, started in Guinea and spread to neighbouring countries, infected more than 28 000 people of which over 11 000 died. As the first outbreak in West Africa, and the largest to date, we were interested in studying the factors that might have led to it. 

There is not much epidemiological data available on Ebola; therefore, [we used a knowledge-based approach](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0009683) to map Ebola virus spillover suitability in regions in Guinea, Gabon and Congo where Ebola outbreaks have occurred. Environmental, climatic and anthropogenic risk factors were identified and their relative importance assessed from a literature review. Spatial data representing each of the identified factors was processed to build the final risk map for each region. A sensitivity analysis suggested that the relevance of the risk factors that are likely involved in the spillover of the virus differs between Guinea and Central Africa. Guinea has a more fragmented landscape with less forest cover compared to Congo and Gabon, and is more densely populated. Thus, the mechanisms that drive spillover might differ between these regions due to differences in the landscape, animal assemblages and animal and human behaviour. As Ebola outbreaks have become more frequent in recent years, identifying the factors that promote the transmission from wild animals to humans is relevant for preventing future outbreaks.
